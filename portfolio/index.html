Laser ablation test rig

The problem

Leaf layers, grease and other residues on rail cut wheel–rail adhesion, causing slips, delays and cancellations. To prove laser cleaning works and improve it, LPS needed a repeatable way to measure how much contamination is removed and how adhesion recovers.

Building the test rig

I built the rig frame from aluminium profile, sourced the components, and instrumented it with optical and laser-based sensors to measure layer thickness, tribology and adhesion before and after laser treatment.

Fixing uneven contamination

Readings varied from run to run. By measuring layer thickness and testing one variable at a time, I traced it to the contamination layer: rolling it on by hand left it patchy and uneven.

So I designed a weighted roller that runs on the ring and applies the same pressure every time. The first version was built from spare workshop parts and two 5 kg weight plates, to prove the idea before refining it.

Readings became consistent, and the roller is now the standard method.

Measuring friction

I made a stand to hold the tribometer on the ring rig, so friction is measured at the same position on every run.

The result

A standard, repeatable method that removes 25 µm of contamination in a single pass, now being extended toward 50 µm.

My role

Technical lead, from building the rig and fixtures to the final client report.

Automated vision system for a rotating test rig (in progress)

The problem

Compressed leaf layers are the classic autumn adhesion problem. To study how they build up and how well the laser removes them, I needed to photograph exactly the same 16 positions on a rotating ring at every stage of a test, with lighting and camera settings identical every time.

Building the camera system

Mounting it on the rig

How it works

  • Marker detection: 16 ArUco markers around the ring identify each position by ID.
  • Closed-loop capture: the camera tracks the markers in real time as the ring turns and triggers a photo once a marker is centred and has held still for several frames, so every image is sharp and lines up with the same spot.
  • Repeatable images: exposure, gain and white balance are locked, and each run records its camera setup and refuses to mix setups within a run.
  • Lighting correction: each image is normalised against a white reference card on the mount, so changes in room light don't skew results.
  • Analysis: Python (OpenCV) measures contamination coverage against each marker's own clean baseline, combined with gauge thickness readings, and generates contact sheets and reports automatically.

Fluence testing

The laser beam's energy is concentrated at the centre of the cleaning band and falls off towards the edges. Because the camera measures coverage across the whole band, the study also tests whether the centre of the band cleans better than the edges.

Status

First run completed in November 2026, with further runs underway.

My role

I designed the study, built the camera system and mounts, and wrote the capture and analysis software.

Turbojet acoustic testing (University of Glasgow)

The problem

Find where the noise from a Cussons P9010e turbojet comes from, and how it changes with thrust.

What I did

  • Recorded the engine at 148 N and 192 N thrust with a scientific microphone, sampling at 40,000 samples per second per channel.
  • Converted the signal to pressure, then to sound pressure level and RMS in decibels.
  • Verified the FFT method in MATLAB on a known square wave first, finding the 262 Hz fundamental and its odd harmonics.
  • Identified the blade passing frequency (11,478 Hz) and calculated engine speed from the spacing of the neighbouring peaks.

The result

The propulsive jet is the main noise source. Noise rises with thrust, because higher jet velocity creates a stronger shear layer.

Otter Pal (in progress)

An always-on Raspberry Pi display for my wall: a pixel-art otter whose world follows the real time, weather and seasons. Every graphic is drawn in code with pygame.

What it does

  • Live weather from the Open-Meteo API: sun, cloud, rain, storms, snow, fog and night skies, plus seasons and holidays. The otter dresses for the conditions.
  • Camera: simple motion detection notices when someone walks in and waves, and the otter waves back when you wave at it.
  • A web page on my home Wi-Fi where anyone can add birthdays or send the otter a message from their phone. It throws a party on birthdays.
  • Daily rhythm: it sleeps from 22:00 to 08:00, with a yawn and a morning stretch, and shows a cycling-weather score.
  • Runs as a system service on the Pi, so it starts on boot and restarts itself if anything crashes.

These scenes were rendered straight from the code using its snapshot mode. Next step: mounting it in a frame on the wall.

Raspberry Pi photo booth

A photo booth for parties, built on a Raspberry Pi 4 with a camera and a monitor. Click once and it takes a strip of four photos with a countdown, then shows the finished strip.

How it works

  • Live camera preview with an overlay, using Picamera2 and PyQt5, full-screen with the cursor hidden.
  • Click → 3-second countdown → four photos → a "making your strip" screen → the strip shown for 8 seconds → back to idle.
  • Every graphic (the glitter frame, hearts, butterflies, flip phone, lip gloss and pocket pet) is drawn in Python with Pillow, with no image files.
  • The photo window uses the same crop on screen and on the strip, so what guests see is exactly what they get. Early versions cut faces in half, and this fixed it.
  • Every photo and strip is saved with a timestamp.
